Welcome

What is retrieval — and why use it?

How can we help students remember and use what they have learned?

In this short course, we'll explore retrieval: what it is, why it can support learning, and practical ways to build it into teaching.

By the end, you'll be able to:
  • explain what retrieval means;
  • distinguish retrieval from simply revisiting material;
  • identify different ways to use retrieval in your teaching;
  • consider when previous learning should be retrieved.

Estimated time: 10–15 minutes. A separate knowledge check is available on the VLE after the course.

Setting the scene

Rosenshine in 60 seconds

Barak Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction brought together findings from three complementary areas of research.

A recurring theme is helping learners build, practise and connect knowledge so that it becomes readily accessible when they need it.

The wider framework

Zooming in

Principle 1: Review previous learning

“Daily review can strengthen previous learning and can lead to fluent recall.”

Rosenshine argues that effective teachers regularly reactivate knowledge students have encountered previously.

Before revealing more, what do you remember about why Rosenshine thought review was important?

Previous knowledge needs to be available when students need it for today's learning. Review can strengthen recall, support fluency and make relevant prior knowledge easier to access.

Why it matters

Why bring knowledge back to mind?

Rosenshine emphasises that previous knowledge should become readily accessible.

When important knowledge can be recalled fluently, students do not have to devote as much of their limited working-memory capacity to reconstructing the basics.

1. Retrieve
Bring relevant previous learning back to mind.
2. Access
Repeated access helps important knowledge become more fluent and readily available.
3. Connect
Available prior knowledge can be connected to new ideas more easily.
4. Build
More working-memory capacity can be devoted to understanding and applying new learning.

RETRIEVE → ACCESS → CONNECT → BUILD
Retrieval is not only about remembering old learning. It can help prepare students for what comes next.

Choose the right form

Retrieval can take many forms

The best technique depends on what you want students to retrieve.

Words & concepts

Brain dump, word cloud, flashcards, quick definitions.

Processes

Rebuild a sequence, complete missing steps, reorder a method.

Connections

Concept map, odd one out, compare and connect.

Explanations

Teach a partner, record a 30-second explanation, summarise aloud.

Misconceptions

Spot the mistake, correct a flawed example, explain why an answer fails.

Digital retrieval

Polls, collaborative boards, flashcards or an AI questioner.

The technology isn't the retrieval. The thinking is.

Example AI prompt: “Ask me questions one at a time about [topic]. Don't give me the answer. If I struggle, give me a hint.”

Designing it well

What makes retrieval useful?

RelevantRetrieve knowledge students will actually need.
From memoryAsk learners to recall, not simply recognise.
InclusiveGive everyone an opportunity to participate.
Low stakesLet students attempt answers without penalty for forgetting.
FeedbackMake sure students can establish the correct or complete answer.
PurposefulUse the activity to learn something useful about what students know.

Over time

What could you bring back — and when?

Imagine students learn an important concept today. It could be retrieved later today, next lesson, next week, next month, or whenever it becomes relevant again.

We don't need to retrieve everything, every time. The aim is to deliberately revisit important knowledge that students will need to retain and use.

Course complete

Bring learning back

Retrieval is not a particular app, quiz or starter activity. It is the act of asking learners to bring previously learned knowledge back to mind.

  • Rosenshine's Principle 1 emphasises short review of previous learning.
  • Retrieval is different from simply re-exposing students to information.
  • There are many ways to retrieve — not just quizzes.
  • Retrieval should focus on useful, relevant knowledge.
  • Principle 10 reminds us to revisit learning over longer periods.
