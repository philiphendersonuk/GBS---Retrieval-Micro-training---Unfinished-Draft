Welcome

What is retrieval — and why use it?

How can we help students remember and use what they have learned?

In this short course, we'll explore retrieval: what it is, why it can support learning, and practical ways to build it into teaching.

By the end, you'll be able to:
  • explain what retrieval means;
  • distinguish retrieval from simply revisiting material;
  • identify different ways to use retrieval in your teaching;
  • consider when previous learning should be retrieved.

Estimated time: 10–15 minutes. A separate knowledge check is available on the VLE after the course.

Setting the scene

Rosenshine in 60 seconds

In the 1980s, Professor of Educational Psychology Barak Rosenshine began researching the characteristics and functions of effective teaching.

In 2012, he published the widely cited Principles of Instruction: Research-Based Strategies That All Teachers Should Know, setting out ten principles for effective instruction.

The principles drew upon three main areas of research…

A recurring theme is helping learners build, practise and connect knowledge so that it becomes readily accessible when they need it.

The wider framework

Rosenshine's 10 principles at a glance

Here are Rosenshine’s ten Principles of Instruction at a glance. Notice how the very first and very last principles connect directly to the concept of retrieval — the focus of this course.

  1. Begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning.
  2. Present new material in small steps with practice after each step.
  3. Ask a large number of questions and check responses.
  4. Provide models.
  5. Guide student practice.
  6. Check for student understanding.
  7. Obtain a high success rate.
  8. Provide scaffolds for difficult tasks.
  9. Require and monitor independent practice.
  10. Engage students in weekly and monthly review.

Why it matters

Why bring knowledge back to mind?

Rosenshine emphasises that previous knowledge should become readily accessible.

When important knowledge can be recalled fluently, students do not have to devote as much of their limited working-memory capacity to reconstructing the basics.

1. Retrieve
Bring relevant previous learning back to mind.
2. Access
Repeated access helps important knowledge become more fluent and readily available.
3. Connect
Available prior knowledge can be connected to new ideas more easily.
4. Build
More working-memory capacity can be devoted to understanding and applying new learning.

RETRIEVE → ACCESS → CONNECT → BUILD
Retrieval is not only about remembering old learning. It can help prepare students for what comes next.

Choose the right form

Retrieval can take many forms

The best technique depends on what you want students to retrieve. Look at some of the examples below.

Words & concepts

Brain dump, word cloud, flashcards, quick definitions.

Processes

Rebuild a sequence, complete missing steps, reorder a method.

Connections

Concept map, odd one out, compare and connect.

Explanations

Teach a partner, record a 30-second explanation, summarise aloud.

Misconceptions

Spot the mistake, correct a flawed example, explain why an answer fails.

Digital retrieval

Polls, collaborative boards, flashcards or an AI questioner.

Remember! When using technology or asking students to use technology, the technology isn't the retrieval. The thinking is.

For example:

✕ Do not ask AI to: Briefly summarise Transformational Leadership and Transactional Leadership for me.

✓ Instead, ask AI to: Give me 5 simple multiple choice questions to check my understanding of Transformational Leadership and Transactional Leadership.

Designing it well

What makes retrieval useful?

Effective retrieval should focus on relevant knowledge students will need, require learners to bring it back from memory, and give everyone an opportunity to participate. Keep it low stakes, provide feedback so students can establish the correct or complete answer, and use their responses purposefully to understand what they know and what may need revisiting.

Over time

Bring important learning back over time

Remember, Rosenshine’s 10th principle also focused on retrieval:

“Engage students in weekly and monthly review.”

Repeated review helps learners develop knowledge that is well connected and readily available. What could you bring back, and when? Imagine students learn an important concept today. It could be retrieved later today, next lesson, next week, next month, or whenever it becomes relevant again.

Course complete

Bring learning back

Retrieval is not a particular app, quiz or starter activity. It is the act of asking learners to bring previously learned knowledge back to mind.

  • Rosenshine's Principle 1 emphasises short review of previous learning.
  • Retrieval is different from simply re-exposing students to information.
  • There are many ways to retrieve — not just quizzes.
  • Retrieval should focus on useful, relevant knowledge.
  • Principle 10 reminds us to revisit learning over longer periods.
